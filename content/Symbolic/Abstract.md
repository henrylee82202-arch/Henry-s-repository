# *Oulipo: A Primer of Potential Literature*—Warren F. Motte Jr.

The poet Raymond Queneau said of himself and the motley writers working under the aegis of OULIPO—an acronym decoded in english as "Workshop of Potential Literature"—as *rats who construct the labyrinth from which they plan to escape.* Splicing together techniques from disciplines as rigorous as set theory and combinatorics, OULIPO strives to make formal constraint its own art with texts that wind like misleading corridors in a haunted house of abstraction.

## Why I love this cover
 
A few reasons why this cover works for me in such a visceral way: 

* As adumbrated by the quote above, I think the cover evokes the feeling of encountering something dangerous and unknowable, and opening the book feels like working your way out of, or into, the seduction of a trap.
* The Torture Cube itself, as if torn from the pages of the Hellraiser franchise, with its eerie backlight and spectral, Francis Bacon-esque cagework limning the facets. I imagine it housing a machine that processes your soul, hollowing it out for demons to wear like pajamas.
* Typology adds an additional sharpness to the already blood-drawing geometry of the cover. Very clean. Very proportionate.

### Excerpts from "Oulipo"

> “The word ‘potential’ concerns the very nature of literature, that is, fundamentally it’s less a question of literature strictly speaking than of supplying forms for the good use one can make of literature. We call potential literature the search for new forms and structures which may be used by writers in any way they see fit."

> The verses corresponding to the arcs arriving at the same point (or leaving from the same point) were chosen in function of a very precise constraint; for example, those that end up at point D contain the word “man” ; those leaving from point D have the same grammatical structure, etc. . . . Using this figure, the reader may choose a priori the point of departure and the point of arrival, and look for “the shortest path.” He can also construct “Hamiltonian Poems,” which correspond to an itinerary in which each point is encountered once and only once. Thus, the Hamiltonian Path BADC gives: “No no says the offended lady I am not looking for the man who spits in the pitcher.” One can even construct quasi-Eulerian poems, traveling through the figure without passing twice by the same arc, and in maximizing the number of arcs used; fundamental, purely mathematical concepts from the Theory of Graphs furnish thus so many constraints. . . and the number of texts that may be constructed using the same figure is infinite!
